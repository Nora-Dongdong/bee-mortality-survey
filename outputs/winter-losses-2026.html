Beekeeper survey · Winter 2025–2026 · The Netherlands

One in five Dutch bee colonies died this winter. Most of the gap is in how they were treated and fed.

Losses were the same as last year and twice the normal level. The beekeepers who lost the fewest colonies treated varroa across the whole season and did not winter their bees on honey alone.

Mingtong Zhang, University of Amsterdam, for the Bee Foundation and the Nederlandse Bijenhouders Vereniging. Survey of 832 apiaries, 695 beekeepers, 4,846 colonies, collected 20 Jan – 7 Apr 2026.

What the data supports

Four decisions

  1. Push full-season varroa treatment. Treating in winter only or summer only went with losses as high as not treating at all.
  2. Advise against wintering on honey alone. Honey-only colonies lost twice as many, unless they also had a full varroa treatment.
  3. Make mite counting routine. Beekeepers who saw no mites lost the most colonies.
  4. Fix the gaps in next year's survey: under-sampled provinces, missing locations, and a sharper pesticide measure.
Finding 1 · Varroa treatment

Half a treatment did no better than none

Colonies that got the full three-step treatment, or a complete alternative treatment, lost 13–19%. Colonies treated in only one season lost 28–32%, more than colonies that were not treated at all.

So: the message to beekeepers is "treat in summer and in winter". Treating once is not a safe middle ground.

Finding 2 · Winter feed

Honey-only colonies lost twice as many, unless they were fully treated

Colonies wintered only on their own honey lost 41%, against about 20% for colonies that also got sugar water. The grid below splits this by treatment. In the honey column, the one low number is the fully treated group.

So: beekeepers who want to winter on honey can, if they treat fully. Those who don't treat fully should add sugar water.

After accounting for treatment and beekeeping method together, honey-only feeding still carried 60–75% higher odds of loss. One cell needs care: sugar-and-honey colonies with no treatment lost only 16%. That group may include beekeepers breeding mite-resistant bees, which the survey did not ask about.

Finding 3 · Beekeeping method

Natural beekeeping looks risky only because of the feeding and treatment that come with it

Natural beekeepers lost 27.6% and controlled-method beekeepers (Aalster, Renson) 13.6%. But natural beekeepers more often winter on honey and skip varroa treatment. Once those choices are accounted for, natural and controlled methods carry similar risk. Regular (regulier) beekeeping, the most common method, still carries about 50% higher odds of loss than controlled methods.

So: advice can focus on treatment and feed for every group, without arguing about the method itself.

Finding 4 · Monitoring

Beekeepers who saw no mites lost the most colonies

Apiaries reporting no varroa lost 31%; those reporting some lost about 20%. Mites are almost always present, so "none seen" most likely means "not looked for". Beekeepers who reported no Asian hornets also lost more (26%), likely for the same reason.

So: promote regular mite counts (sticky board or sugar roll). They turn an invisible problem into a treatment decision.

Finding 5 · The land around the apiary

More kinds of crops nearby went with slightly lower losses; the pesticide estimate showed no harm

Higher estimated pesticide use did not go with higher losses. The estimate counts all pesticides by weight, so it cannot single out the ones harmful to bees.

So: do not read this as "pesticides are safe". It says the current measure is too coarse. A bee-specific measure is the next step.

Before next winter

What would make next year's answer stronger

  • Recruit where the survey is thin. Zeeland had 7 apiaries, Friesland 11, Groningen 18. Province results also differ from the WUR survey, so province-level rankings are not reliable yet.
  • Ask for a location in every response. Only 427 of 832 apiaries gave one, which halves the sample for the land analysis.
  • Ask about mite-resistant breeding and mite counts. Both would explain the odd results in findings 2 and 4.
  • Narrow the pesticide measure to compounds known to harm bees, and follow the same apiaries across years.
How sure we are

Strong enough to act on, not proof of cause

  • Beekeepers volunteered and reported their own numbers. The survey covers about 5% of the estimated 80,000–100,000 Dutch colonies.
  • These are associations. Beekeepers who treat fully may also do other things well.
  • The key comparisons were checked with a model that treats each beekeeper's apiaries as related (GEE), so a few large beekeepers cannot drive the results.
